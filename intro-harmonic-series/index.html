A short listening lab

One sound, four musical intervals.

A single musical pitch is not always one vibration. Its faster partials create the octave, perfect fifth, major third, and minor third you already meet in theory class.

The point

Listen first. Name it second.

We are not trying to memorize formulas here. We are hearing why octaves, fifths, and thirds keep returning in music.

01 / vibrationBuild one sound from its partials.

Start with C2.

C2 vibrates at about 65 Hz. The lines below do not show notation; they show how many vibration cycles fit into the same stretch of time. Reveal a few, then hear the stack.

02 / intervalsThese are the early relationships worth recognizing.

The first six partials already give us a lot of music theory.

Each card compares two nearby partials. Listen to the lower pitch, then the higher pitch, then both together. You do not need to use the word “ratio” to notice how different these interval relationships feel.

03 / mixChoose the ingredients you want to hear.

Make a tiny overtone mix.

Turn partials on and off, then listen. The sound changes because its ingredients change. This is a simplified version of what helps distinguish timbres.

You are hearing partial 1: the fundamental, C2.

Bonus / laterOnly if you are curious about the math.
Optional vocabulary

Ratios are a concise way to describe what you just heard.

“2:1” means one pitch vibrates twice as fast as another. They are not the goal of this activity, but they are a helpful label once the sound relationships make sense.

2:1octave
3:2perfect fifth
5:4major third
6:5minor third
